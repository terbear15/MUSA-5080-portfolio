Week 5: Linear Regression for Prediction

Building rent models, checking residuals, and testing predictions with cross-validation.
Published

September 25, 2026

What We Did

We used linear regression to predict median rent across Philadelphia Census tracts. We started with median household income as one predictor, then added the percentage of renters, educational attainment, and the number of nearby Indego stations.

We learned how to interpret regression coefficients, predictions, and residuals. We also compared model fit with prediction accuracy using a train/test split and cross-validation. Finally, we mapped the residuals to see where rents were higher or lower than the model expected.

Key Concepts

Outcomes, predictors, and noise

A statistical model can be written as:

\[ Y = f(X) + \varepsilon \]

  • \(Y\) is the outcome we want to explain or predict, such as median rent.
  • \(X\) contains the predictors, such as income or educational attainment.
  • \(f(X)\) is the relationship the model tries to learn.
  • \(\varepsilon\) represents everything affecting rent that is not captured by the model, including random noise and omitted characteristics.

For a simple linear regression:

\[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X + \varepsilon \]

The fitted prediction is:

\[ \hat{Y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1X \]

The intercept, \(\hat{\beta}_0\), is the predicted outcome when the predictor equals zero. The slope, \(\hat{\beta}_1\), describes the predicted change in the outcome associated with a one-unit increase in the predictor.

Prediction and inference

Regression output can be read in two different ways:

  • Inference focuses on \(\hat{\beta}\) and asks how a predictor is associated with the outcome.
  • Prediction focuses on \(\hat{Y}\) and asks how accurately the model can predict an outcome for observations it has not seen.

A coefficient can help prediction without proving causation. For example, the number of Indego stations may help predict rent, but the model cannot prove that adding a station causes rent to increase. Station locations may represent other neighborhood characteristics that the model does not include.

Fitting a linear model in R

A simple rent model can be fitted with:

m1 <- lm(med_rent ~ med_inc, data = tracts)
summary(m1)

The formula can be read as: predict med_rent using med_inc.

In our class example, the estimated income coefficient was approximately 0.0081. This represents about $0.0081 in additional predicted monthly rent for each additional dollar of annual household income. Multiplying by $10,000 makes the result easier to understand:

\[ 0.0081 \times 10{,}000 = 81 \]

A tract with $10,000 more in median household income therefore had a predicted monthly rent approximately $81 higher.

A small p-value provides evidence that an association is statistically detectable. It does not tell us whether the effect is large, whether it is causal, or whether the model predicts new observations well.

Least squares and residuals

Linear regression chooses the line that minimizes the sum of squared errors:

\[ \sum (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2 \]

A residual is the difference between an observed value and its prediction:

\[ e_i = Y_i - \hat{Y}_i \]

  • A positive residual means the actual rent is higher than predicted.
  • A negative residual means the actual rent is lower than predicted.
  • A residual near zero means the prediction is close to the observed rent.

Mapping residuals can reveal spatial patterns. If nearby tracts have similar residuals, the model may be missing a location-based characteristic.

Rigid and flexible models

Models can be arranged from more rigid to more flexible:

More rigid Middle More flexible
Linear regression Lasso and ridge Trees
Logistic regression Random forest Boosting and neural networks

Rigid models are usually easier to explain and interpret. Flexible models can capture more complicated relationships, but they can be harder to understand and may overfit the training data.

A parametric model, such as linear regression, assumes a particular functional form and estimates a fixed set of parameters. A nonparametric model makes fewer assumptions about the functional form and can follow more complicated patterns in the data.

Model fit versus prediction

\(R^2\) measures how much of the variation in the outcome is explained by the model in the data used to fit it. A higher \(R^2\) indicates a better in-sample fit, but it does not guarantee better predictions for new data.

A train/test split separates the observations into two groups:

  • The training set is used to fit the model.
  • The test set is used to evaluate predictions on unseen observations.

In class, we used approximately 80% of the tracts for training and 20% for testing.

Prediction error was measured using root mean squared error:

\[ RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum(Y_i-\hat{Y}_i)^2} \]

RMSE is expressed in the same units as the outcome. For a rent model, an RMSE of $250 means that predictions miss the observed monthly rent by approximately $250. A lower RMSE is better.

Cross-validation

One train/test split can give different results depending on which observations are selected. K-fold cross-validation provides a more stable evaluation.

For five-fold cross-validation:

  1. Divide the observations into five groups.
  2. Hold out one group as the test set.
  3. Train the model using the other four groups.
  4. Calculate the test RMSE.
  5. Repeat until each group has been used for testing.
  6. Average the five RMSE values.

We used the same folds when comparing models so that the comparison was fair.

This use of CV means cross-validation. It is different from the coefficient of variation, which measures the uncertainty of an ACS estimate.

Regression diagnostics

A good residual plot should look like a random cloud around zero.

Homoscedasticity means the residuals have a similar amount of spread across the range of predicted values. Heteroscedasticity means the spread changes, often creating a funnel shape. In that case, one RMSE may hide the fact that the model makes much larger errors for some observations.

Noise in the outcome \(Y\) becomes part of the residuals. Noise in a predictor \(X\) can flatten the estimated slope.

Multicollinearity occurs when predictors are strongly related to each other. It can make individual coefficients unstable and difficult to interpret. For a prediction task, the main question is whether the variables improve prediction accuracy on unseen data, which can be checked with cross-validation.

Proving R Works

This example creates a small tract-level rent dataset and fits a linear model that predicts median rent using median household income.

library(tidyverse)

rent_data <- tibble(
  GEOID = c("100", "200", "300", "400", "500", "600"),
  med_rent = c(1050, 1200, 1380, 1500, 1725, 1900),
  med_inc = c(35000, 48000, 62000, 75000, 92000, 110000),
  pct_renters = c(71, 65, 59, 54, 48, 42),
  pct_ba = c(18, 25, 34, 42, 55, 68),
  n_stations = c(1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5)
)

m1 <- lm(med_rent ~ med_inc, data = rent_data)

summary(m1)

Reflection

I learned that fitting the data and predicting new observations are related but different goals. A model can have a higher \(R^2\) because it fits the existing observations better, but cross-validation is needed to determine whether it also predicts unseen tracts better. I also understand that a coefficient describes an association while a residual identifies where the actual outcome differs from the model’s prediction.

Mapping the residuals was especially useful because it showed that model errors may form geographic clusters. If nearby tracts have rents that are consistently higher than predicted, the model may be missing a spatial feature such as access to Center City, universities, transit, newer housing, or other neighborhood amenities.

Questions I Still Have

  • How do I decide whether an improvement in cross-validation RMSE is large enough to justify adding another predictor?
  • If the residual map shows geographic clusters, what spatial variables should I add to the model?
  • When predictors are strongly correlated, how can I tell whether their individual coefficients are still useful to interpret?
