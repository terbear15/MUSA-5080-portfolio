Week 8: LISA and Spatial Feature Engineering

Finding where model errors cluster and creating spatial features to improve predictions.
Published

October 23, 2026

What We Did

We moved from Global Moran’s I to Local Moran’s I, also called LISA. Global Moran’s I tells us whether model errors are spatially clustered overall, while LISA shows where specific clusters and spatial outliers are located.

We also compared three ways of measuring proximity: buffer counts, distance to the nearest feature, and average distance to the nearest (k) features. Finally, we discussed the RealPage rent-pricing case and why an accurate model can still create legal, ethical, and social problems.

Key Concepts

A residual is the difference between the actual and predicted value:

\[\text{Residual} = \text{Actual} - \text{Predicted}\]

A positive residual means the model predicted too low. A negative residual means the model predicted too high.

Global Moran’s I produces one number that answers whether errors are spatially clustered. Local Moran’s I, or LISA, produces one result for each observation and shows where clusters are located.

The four LISA categories are:

  • High–High: A house and its neighbors are underpredicted.
  • Low–Low: A house and its neighbors are overpredicted.
  • High–Low: An underpredicted house surrounded by overpredicted houses.
  • Low–High: An overpredicted house surrounded by underpredicted houses.

High–High and Low–Low are spatial clusters. High–Low and Low–High are spatial outliers. Clusters may point to missing neighborhood features, while outliers may result from something unusual about an individual house.

Measuring What Is Nearby

There are three common ways to turn proximity into a model feature:

  1. A buffer count counts features within a chosen distance.
  2. A nearest distance measures distance to the closest feature.
  3. A k-nearest-neighbor distance averages the distance to the closest (k) features.

The buffer radius and the value of (k) are choices that need to be explained. Reasonable alternatives can be compared using cross-validation RMSE.

A log transformation can be useful for distance because changing from 100 to 500 feet may matter more than changing from 5,000 to 5,400 feet.

Proving R Works

This small example classifies houses into LISA quadrants using each house’s standardized residual, its neighbors’ average residual, and a significance level of 0.01.

library(tidyverse)

lisa_example <- tibble(
  house = c("A", "B", "C", "D", "E"),
  residual_z = c(84, 64, 4, -66, -86),
  neighbor_lag = c(64, 44, -1, -41, -66),
  p_value = c(0.004, 0.008, 0.400, 0.006, 0.002)
)

lisa_example <- lisa_example %>%
  mutate(
    lisa_group = case_when(
      p_value >= 0.01 ~ "Not significant",
      residual_z > 0 & neighbor_lag > 0 ~ "High-High",
      residual_z < 0 & neighbor_lag < 0 ~ "Low-Low",
      residual_z > 0 & neighbor_lag < 0 ~ "High-Low",
      TRUE ~ "Low-High"
    )
  )

lisa_example
# A tibble: 5 × 5
  house residual_z neighbor_lag p_value lisa_group     
  <chr>      <dbl>        <dbl>   <dbl> <chr>          
1 A             84           64   0.004 High-High      
2 B             64           44   0.008 High-High      
3 C              4           -1   0.4   Not significant
4 D            -66          -41   0.006 Low-Low        
5 E            -86          -66   0.002 Low-Low

House A is High–High because it has a positive residual and its neighbors also have positive residuals. House D is Low–Low because it and its neighbors have negative residuals. House C is not significant, so there is not enough evidence to treat it as a meaningful spatial pattern.

Improving a Spatial Model

The general process is:

  1. Generate out-of-fold predictions and residuals.
  2. Check Global Moran’s I.
  3. Make a LISA map.
  4. Identify significant High–High or Low–Low clusters.
  5. Ask what spatial feature the model may be missing.
  6. Add a measurable feature.
  7. Compare CV RMSE, Moran’s I, and the LISA map again.

Lower CV RMSE means better prediction. Moran’s I closer to zero means the remaining errors are less spatially clustered.

The RealPage Case

RealPage recommends rents using market information and data supplied by landlords. The main controversy is not simply whether its regression model is accurate. It concerns the use of competitors’ nonpublic rental data and how strongly landlords are encouraged to follow the recommendations.

The case raises three questions:

  1. What data enters the model, and who supplied it?
  2. Who acts on the prediction, and can they reject it?
  3. Who is affected when the prediction is wrong—or when it is accurate?

An accurate prediction of what tenants are willing to pay can still contribute to higher rents or unfair coordination.

Reflection

I learned that Global Moran’s I can warn me that errors are clustered, but LISA is more useful for identifying the particular places where a model is failing. Those clusters can guide feature engineering by showing where the model may be missing a neighborhood characteristic.

I also learned that improving predictive accuracy is not the only concern. The RealPage example shows that the source of the data, the people making decisions from the prediction, and the people affected by those decisions are also important.

Questions I Still Have

  • How should I decide whether a LISA cluster is large enough to investigate?
  • How do I choose an appropriate buffer radius or number of nearest neighbors?
  • Can adding a feature improve CV RMSE while making spatial inequality worse?
